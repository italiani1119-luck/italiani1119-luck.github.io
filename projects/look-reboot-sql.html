01 / Brief

Six questions, one SQL brief.

The brief ran on theLook eCommerce, a public retail dataset on Google BigQuery: eight years of apparel orders across customers, products, and 14 markets. The task was to answer the questions a leadership team would actually ask before planning Q4 — using queries, not opinions.

The deck had to answer six questions in order: is growth real, and where does it come from? Which categories carry the business? Are the premium prices genuine? Who buys — first-time or repeat customers? What do returns really cost? And which markets are at risk of losing their customers?

I worked from the transaction tables out: orders and order_items for value, products for category and price, customers for geography. Every number in the brief is a query result, and every recommendation is tied to one of those results.

Two habits shaped the work. First, check the data before recommending anything — one pricing question turned into a data-quality flag. Second, state the assumptions on the page, so a manager can see exactly what a number includes and what it leaves out.

Scope and assumptions

  • Period: 2019 to October 2026. 2026 covers ten months, so its year-on-year figure is marked partial.
  • Orders: all statuses included in the growth and customer analyses; returns measured separately.
  • Value: item-level sale_price summed from order_items; order value divided by order count gives AOV.
  • Markets: countries with fewer than 100 customers are shown but not compared.

02 / Sales pulse

Steady growth, not a spike.

The first question was whether sales are spiky or structural. Grouping every order by year shows a climb from $87K in 2019 to $2.48M in 2025 — with Q4 consistently the strongest stretch.

AOV has held between $84.84 and $87.06 for eight years — growth is coming from order volume, not from a higher basket.

What the data says

  • Growth is structural. Sales rose from $87K to $2.48M between 2019 and 2025, up 45% in 2024 and 48% in 2025.
  • Q4 is the peak. October to December runs 16–18% above the yearly average.
  • New buyers carry it. Order value stays near $86, so growth comes from more orders, not bigger ones.
  • Repeat is starting. Orders per buyer reached 1.12 in September 2026 — an early sign of repeat buying.

Recommendation: stock up for Q4 early rather than reacting to demand.

Bet the winners, fix the laggards.

Which categories deserve the Q4 stock and the campaign space? I joined item-level sales to the product table, grouped by category, and ranked 2024 by revenue.

What the data says

  • Two categories carry the mix. Outerwear ($200K) and Jeans ($193K) made up about 54% of the top five's sales.
  • Jeans nearly won. It sold 44% more items at a 33% lower price than Outerwear.
  • Swim moves volume, not value. 1,755 items at the lowest price in the group ($56).
  • Suits sell rarely. Only 816 items at $129 — the smallest base of the five.

Recommendation: push the top two, bundle Swim to raise basket size, and investigate why Suits underperforms.

04 / Price check

Premium, or a data error?

A window function adds each category's average price to every product, then ranks products by how far above that average they sit. The top 20 came back with a pattern that changed the question.

Categories of the 20 products furthest above their category average. Price difference is retail price minus the category average across all products.
Category SKUs in top 20 Category average Max price
Active7$50.62$903
Outerwear & Coats4$146.02$999
Shorts3$45.77$999
Socks2$20.42$903
Four other categories4—$903

Very high prices also pull their own category average up, so a plain difference-over-average ranks the extreme values first.

The finding

  • 14 of 20 outliers share one price. Socks, sports bras, and jackets all sit at exactly $903 — which is not a pricing strategy.
  • It reads as a data-entry error. Different products, one round number, same result across categories.
  • The useful output is a check. Validate those SKUs before any pricing decision is made from this table.

Recommendation: fix the source records, then re-run the outlier query before repricing anything.

  • New customers dominate. First orders bring 63.9% of sales: 80,131 orders and $6.9M.
  • Repeat spend is flat. AOV is $86.26 for first orders and $86.93 for repeat — almost identical.
  • Repeat customers return, not upsell. About 0.56 repeat orders per customer.

Recommendation: protect acquisition, then use bundles or loyalty offers to lift the second and third order.

06 / Returns

The hidden cost: $1.06M in returns.

Returned orders are grouped by category; the return rate is returned value divided by that category's sales. The result is not one bad category — it is a company-wide pattern.

  • Suits run highest. An 11.5% rate, but from a much smaller sales base.

Recommendation: start fit and quality checks on Jeans and Outerwear, and identify which models need a redesign.

08 / Method

Joins, groups, and window functions.

Four tables, one BigQuery dataset. The work is ordinary SQL done carefully: join on the right keys, aggregate at the right grain, and never average an average.

Category ranking

WITH category_sales AS (
  SELECT p.category, oi.sale_price, p.retail_price
  FROM `bigquery-public-data.thelook_ecommerce.order_items` AS oi
  JOIN `bigquery-public-data.thelook_ecommerce.products` AS p
    ON oi.product_id = p.id
  WHERE EXTRACT(YEAR FROM oi.created_at) = 2024
)
SELECT category,
       ROUND(SUM(sale_price), 2)   AS sales,
       COUNT(*)                    AS items,
       ROUND(AVG(retail_price), 2) AS avg_price
FROM category_sales
GROUP BY category
ORDER BY sales DESC
LIMIT 5;

Price outliers

WITH product_prices AS (
  SELECT id AS product_id, name, category, retail_price,
         AVG(retail_price) OVER (
           PARTITION BY category
         ) AS avg_category_price
  FROM `bigquery-public-data.thelook_ecommerce.products`
)
SELECT product_id,
       name,
       category,
       retail_price,
       IF(retail_price > avg_category_price, 1, 0) AS is_above_avg,
       ROUND(retail_price - avg_category_price, 2) AS delta
FROM product_prices
ORDER BY delta DESC
LIMIT 20;

First-time vs repeat, without double counting

WITH order_value AS (
  SELECT order_id, SUM(sale_price) AS order_total
  FROM `bigquery-public-data.thelook_ecommerce.order_items`
  GROUP BY order_id
),
ranked AS (
  SELECT order_id,
         ROW_NUMBER() OVER (
           PARTITION BY user_id
           ORDER BY created_at, order_id
         ) AS order_seq
  FROM `bigquery-public-data.thelook_ecommerce.orders`
)
SELECT CASE WHEN r.order_seq = 1 THEN 'First'
            ELSE 'Repeat' END AS order_type,
       COUNT(*) AS orders,
       ROUND(SUM(v.order_total), 2) AS sales,
       ROUND(SUM(v.order_total) / COUNT(*), 2) AS aov
FROM ranked AS r
JOIN order_value AS v
  ON v.order_id = r.order_id
GROUP BY order_type;

Order value is aggregated before the join. Joining items directly would repeat each order row and inflate both the order count and the AOV denominator.

Data-quality checks

  • One impossible pattern. 14 of 20 price outliers at $903 triggered a source check before any pricing conclusion.
  • Overlapping categories. Suits and Suits & Sport Coats both appear in returns, so the two are read separately, not summed.
  • Partial years. 2026 is ten months; its year-on-year figure is labelled and never compared to a full year without the caveat.

Why the read order matters

  • 01
    Trend first. A category decision made on a spiky baseline is a guess.
  • 02
    Then mix. Category ranking shows where volume and value actually sit.
  • 03
    Then people. Customer segments and market health decide what to do with the mix.
  • 04
    Then cost of failure. Returns turn the plan into a margin question.
