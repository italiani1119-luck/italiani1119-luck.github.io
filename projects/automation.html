01 / Brief

The work was reliable only if I remembered it.

Each recurring task was small. Together they took the part of the day meant for real work — and one forgotten reminder could undo a week of planning.

The list was ordinary: a daily LinkedIn post, comment replies, a morning job scan, three reminders, supplier follow-ups, and a video slot. None of it was difficult. All of it was constant, and all of it depended on memory.

The obvious fix was to automate everything. The risk was just as obvious: a machine publishing in my name, applying to jobs, or emailing a supplier without review. Speed without control would have been worse than the manual routine.

So the build followed one rule from the start: automate the preparation, keep the decision. Every workflow produces a draft, a digest, or a reminder. A person approves anything that leaves the machine, and every action writes a record.

The five recurring tasks

  • Daily LinkedIn post and comment triage
  • Morning scan for remote job leads
  • Three daily reminders and completion logging
  • Procurement paperwork from requisition to payment
  • Short-video production and scheduled publishing

02 / Workflows

Five workflows, one human checkpoint each.

Each runs on a schedule or a trigger. None of them acts outward without an approval or an explicit read-only rule.

01

LinkedIn content engine

A daily draft at 08:00, an email preview, then explicit approve, revise, or cancel. Only an approved draft publishes at its scheduled slot; a record-and-cleanup step closes the loop.

02

Job-lead digest

Every 08:00, five to ten verified remote roles posted within the last seven days. It is read-only by design: the digest never applies to a role and never contacts an employer.

03

Reminder and task tracker

Three reminders a day at 04:00, 08:00, and 16:00 WIB. A one-click “done” link marks the task complete, the log writes itself, and a monthly recap shows what actually happened.

04

Procurement chatbot

Covers the full cycle from requisition to payment, and drafts RFQs, vendor criteria, and contract checklists by email — for a buyer to review, not to send on its own.

05

Local video pipeline

Seventy-second educational and thirty-second affiliate videos with narration, captions, scheduled publishing, and a duplicate ledger so the same clip is never posted twice.

Where control sits

  • A status field. Every draft moves from pending to approved to publishing to posted. No post skips a state.
  • An atomic claim. Publishing is claimed before the API call, so two runs cannot post the same draft twice.
  • A local store. SQLite holds the queue, the timestamps, and the LinkedIn post ID — readable without a vendor dashboard.

Why the build is local

  • 01 The Flask dashboard runs on 127.0.0.1:8080 with a login and CSRF protection.
  • 02 API keys and OAuth tokens stay on the machine, not in a third-party workflow builder.
  • 03 A short-lived tunnel carries only the approval link and the OAuth callback.

04 / Guardrails

The rules came before the workflows.

Four constraints decided what the system was allowed to do. Everything else was built inside them.

Guardrails

  • 01 No post without explicit approval.
  • 02 No auto-applying to jobs.
  • 03 API keys stay on the local machine.
  • 04 Every action leaves a log.
View the architecture diagram

Approval is a state, not a promise. The publishing job only picks up posts whose status is approved and whose publish time has passed. If nobody approves, nothing goes out — the queue grows and the morning preview shows it.

Read-only is enforced, not trusted. The job digest has no send path, and the procurement assistant produces drafts for a buyer to carry into email. Limiting the tool was easier than policing its output.

Logging is part of the work. Reminder completions, approvals, rewrites, cancellations, and post IDs all land in the same SQLite file. The monthly recap is a report over that file, not a separate spreadsheet.

Credentials never leave the machine. The dashboard is bound to localhost, and OAuth tokens are exchanged and stored locally. The tunnel exists for the approval page and callback only.

If I cannot see who approved what, the automation is not saving time — it is moving risk.
