01 / Brief

Screen time that gives something back.

Most games for this age ask the child to watch. I wanted one that asks the child to do something — and shows a grown-up what happened.

For a three-to-six-year-old, screen time usually means a video plays and the child watches. It is hard to tell what was learned, and it depends on a connection that is not always there.

The brief was deliberately narrow: a small game with real learning content, playable without wifi, that keeps progress between sessions and gives a parent a way to see it. A car seat, a waiting room, or a dead spot at home are exactly when a game needs to work.

The result is a Montessori-inspired app with five trails, three age bands, and a grown-ups area. It installs like a native app, plays offline, and was built end-to-end with Qoder as the coding partner.

Design constraints

  • Playable with no connection
  • Progress saved on the device
  • Readable on a 320 px screen
  • Montessori areas as the content spine
  • A progress view a grown-up can read
  • Installable on Android and iOS

02 / Method

Five trails. Three age bands.

The content follows the Montessori areas rather than a generic quiz ladder. Each trail gets a wider challenge as the child moves up an age band.

Three age bands

  • 3–4 years. Recognition and routines, with the simplest choices.
  • 4–5 years. Sorting, matching, and early counting.
  • 5–6 years. Sequences, story order, and more deliberate answers.

Progress a grown-up can read

  • 02 A spark jar that fills with correct answers.
  • 03 A grown-ups area for progress and settings.
A child should not need a connection — or a parent leaning over the screen — to keep learning.

03 / Build

An app that works with the wifi off.

Offline was a requirement, not a fallback. The whole experience — screens, audio, and progress — lives on the device.

The game is a web app with an offline service worker that caches the app shell and the audio. Once it has loaded, play does not wait on the network, and a dropped connection does not interrupt a trail.

Progress is saved locally, so stars, badges, and the spark count survive closing the app. The grown-ups area reads from the same local store — there is no account to create and no server round-trip to see how the child is doing.

It is packaged with Capacitor for Android and iOS as well as running in the browser. The layout was tested on desktop and down to a 320 px viewport, which is the width where small hands and small screens actually meet.

The source is public, including the offline service worker and the build scripts, so the claims on this page can be checked rather than taken on trust.

Progress kept separate from playBadges, stars, and sparks live on the reward shelf; settings sit in the grown-ups area.
Offline by defaultAudio and progress are local, so the game behaves the same on a plane, in a car, or at home.
